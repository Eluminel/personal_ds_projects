Predictive Modelling of Suicide Risk Among Women with Postpartum Depression

Introduction:
Postpartum depression (PPD) is a challenge that affects one out of every eight women across the world with severe instances being highly dangerous as there is a risk of incidence of suicidal thoughts and actions. It interferes with emotional stability and cognitive performance at a time where the patient is already vulnerable, and it is essential to identify and treat it early (Chen et al., 2025). Postpartum suicide is a severe health issue in this contemporary world, and predictive modelling within the same group is an urgent task. This complexity of severe PPD is caused by multifactorial biopsychosocial factors, delays in diagnosis, and stigma to timely care. This requires multi-dimensional approaches that are sensitive and encompass clinical, social, and technological views. This study aligns with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), SDG 4 (Quality Education), SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), SDG 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions), and SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals). These goals underscore a global commitment to equitable health access, education, and collaborative efforts to safeguard maternal mental health (United Nations, n.d.).
The report is organized into five main sections: Introduction, Problem Formulation, Methods, Results & Analyses and Conclusion. The Introduction explains the background and significance of the predictive modelling application. The Problem Formulation formalizes the machine learning task, detailing the dataset, features, and labels. The Methods section describes the dataset size, preprocessing steps, feature selection process, choice of models and loss functions, and the data splitting strategy for training, validation, and testing. The Results and Analysis section presents model performance, compares the chosen methods, and identifies the best-performing model; the Conclusion summarizes the findings, discusses limitations, and outlines future work.

Problem Formulation:
This study formalizes the prediction of suicide risk among women with severe postpartum depression as a supervised machine learning classification problem. The objective is to classify whether a woman is at risk of suicidal behaviour based on features derived from postpartum health and psychological questionnaires. The dataset comprises 1,503 records and 11 columns collected from a medical hospital using a questionnaire administered via Google Forms. Nine features serve as independent variables representing various postpartum symptoms and behavioural indicators, while the target variable, labelled "Suicide attempt," indicates the presence or absence of suicidal risk and acts as the label to be predicted. The source of the dataset is the public repository on Kaggle (Parvez Al-Muqtadir, n.d.). Interested readers are referred to Table 1 for a detailed description of the dataset features and their description. The dataset’s purpose is to provide structured information on postpartum depression-related symptoms to support early detection and intervention efforts.

Methods:
This section gives the methodological framework that will be used in this study in predicting suicide risk in women who have severe postpartum depression. Figure 1 illustrates the general workflow that is followed in the study as it shows the sequential steps of the data acquisition process up to the model interpretation.
The data is a collection of 1,503 records based on a clinical questionnaire that were given in a medical hospital, which were obtained on Kaggle (Parvez Al-Muqtadir, n.d.). All records had 15 attributes, which are associated with postpartum health, and 10 categorical columns (excluding time stamp) were chosen to be included in this work as the most relevant and quality ones, and the target column represents the risk of suicide, which is denoted as the attribute “Suicide attempt”. Pre-processing of the data consisted of the deletion of the timestamp column, as it just depicts the time at which the entries were made into the Google Form, which can be inferred from domain knowledge-based dimensionality reduction. The remaining columns remain relevant for this study. Additionally, records with missing values have been deleted, resulting in 1,491 valid data to model. Class distribution of the values in the target attribute represented in Figure 2 shows there is a large imbalance in classes, which needs to be catered to for fair analyses, done in the absence of bias. Label encoding was used to encode the categorical variables in numerical format that can be used in the machine learning model.
The selection of the features was made to identify the variables that are most strongly related to the symptoms of postpartum depression, as well as the psychological risk factors, which resulted in the final refined dataset that is neither too complex to understand nor too simple to interpret. In the case of the machine learning task, the k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN) algorithm was selected because it utilizes a simple methodology and is effective when the task is to classify a new example based on the labels of similar examples in the feature space, with Euclidean distance as a similarity measure (GeeksforGeeks, 2025). The value of the hyperparameter k=3 was chosen as a balance to reduce the chances of overfitting that can come with low values of k and underfitting that can come with high values of k. Five-fold cross-validation was used for validation as it offers a reliable way to assess the model’s performance by dividing the training data into five subsets and iteratively training on four while validating on the fifth, helping to reduce variability and ensure the model generalizes well. The data was divided randomly into 80% training and 20% testing to provide adequate data for learning patterns and an independent set for objective evaluation. Since it does not rely on a traditional loss function, model performance is evaluated using classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, especially to handle class imbalance.
The kNN training process involves storing the labelled data points without building any explicit model. When making a prediction for a new input vector x, the algorithm finds the three closest neighbours in the feature space by calculating the Euclidean distance, which is the square root of the sum of the squared differences between each corresponding feature of the points. The predicted class ‘y1’ is then assigned based on which class label appears most frequently among these three nearest neighbours. In other words, the model looks at the labels of the three closest points and picks the label that has the majority vote.
Besides the k-Nearest Neighbours model, a Decision Tree classifier was utilised as a supplementary point of view with a variant hypothesis space. The Decision Tree was chosen because it has the capability to represent nonlinear relationships between the features and the target and also provides a mean of interpretation to be viewed in the options of the decision and the contribution of the features. In comparison to kNN, Decision Trees do not depend on any explicit loss function, but rely rather on a
criterion like the Gini impurity or information gain to decide how to split the feature space (Koli, 2021). The two models were trained and tested with the same training and test splits with 80 percent of the data used to train and 20 percent used to test and 5-fold cross-validation was implemented on the training set to make sure of sound performance evaluation and minimize variation.
To measure the performance of the models, two error tracking strategies were used which are complementary to one another. The first was the 0/1 loss (error rate) which was simply a measure of correctness. The second loss, logarithmic loss (cross-entropy), took into consideration the probabilities of the predicted results, which provided a more sensitive analysis of model confidence as well as dealing with skewed distribution of classes. Both models were also developed into confusion matrices and classification reports that gave specific information on the true positive and false negative rates and also the precision, recall and F1-scores. This integrated format enabled an overall evaluation of predictive performance, interpretability and strength between the various modelling strategies and tapping into the domain knowledge in the choice of the pertinent features.

Results:
This section contains the performance analysis of the two machine learning models, k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN) and Decision Tree, when predicting the risk of suicide in women with severe postpartum depression. There are several metrics used to measure model performance, which include 5-fold cross-validation accuracy, training and test accuracy, error rate (0/1 loss), logarithmic loss (cross- entropy) and classification reports. The assessment gives a more sensitive evaluation that considers the predicted probabilities and imbalance of classes as well as the simple measurement of the accuracy of classification. 
The Decision Tree classifier, as depicted in Table 2. (a), demonstrates strong predictive performance across all metrics. Its training accuracy during 5-fold cross-validation is exceptionally high (0.9966), indicating that the model effectively captures patterns in the training data. Validation accuracy remains high at 0.9363, showing that the model generalizes well to unseen folds without severe overfitting. The final model, evaluated on the independent test set, achieves a test accuracy of 0.9632, corresponding to a low 0/1 error rate of 0.0368, which indicates robust generalization to new data. Logarithmic loss, which accounts for prediction probabilities and sensitivity to class imbalance, is 1.1028, reflecting moderate confidence in probability estimates but consistent classification performance. The classification report further confirms the model’s strength, with precision, recall, and F1-scores above 0.92 for all classes, particularly demonstrating high recall for minority classes, which is critical for identifying high-risk individuals. In contrast, k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN), as shown in Table 2. (b), performs reasonably but exhibits lower overall metrics. Cross-validation training accuracy is 0.9545, slightly lower than the Decision Tree, and validation accuracy is 0.8440, suggesting less robust generalization during training. On the independent test set, kNN achieves a test accuracy of 0.8662, with an error rate of 0.1338, indicating that it misclassifies a higher proportion of instances compared to the Decision Tree. Logarithmic loss is marginally lower at 1.0176, reflecting that kNN predictions provide slightly better-calibrated probabilities in some instances, but this does not compensate for its weaker classification performance across metrics. The classification report shows lower precision, recall, and F1-scores compared to the Decision Tree, particularly for certain classes, highlighting its limitations in capturing complex nonlinear relationships in the dataset. 
Comparing the two models, it is evident that the Decision Tree consistently outperforms kNN in terms of overall accuracy, error rate, and class-specific performance, while also providing interpretability through its decision paths. Although kNN offers slightly better probabilistic calibration in terms of log loss, the substantial improvement in correct classification and minority class identification makes the Decision Tree the preferred model for this predictive task. Based on these results, the Decision Tree classifier is selected as the final model for predicting suicide risk in women with severe postpartum depression, effectively balancing predictive performance, generalization, and interpretability, while ensuring reliable identification of high-risk cases.

Conclusion:
Decision Tree classifier performs better than kNN in accuracy, error rate, and class-specific measures, and therefore it is more preferable in predicting risks of suicide among women with severe postpartum depression. However, the log loss is quite big, which suggests that the probabilities that are to be predicted are not fully trustworthy, which is essential in sensitive clinical information. This implies that the model is very effective in the classification of cases however probability estimation can be improved. The next round of work is going to be the enhancement of probability calibration, experimenting with ensemble and hybrid models, adding more clinically relevant features, dealing with class imbalance, and experimenting with other loss functions. These measures are expected to increase the reliability, interpretability, and safety of model use during clinical decision-making and guarantee a strong ability to identify individuals at high risk.
